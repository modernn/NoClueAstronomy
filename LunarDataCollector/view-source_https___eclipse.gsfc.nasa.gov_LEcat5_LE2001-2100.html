<h2 align="center">Introduction</h2>

<p class="quote">Eclipses of the Moon can only occur during the Full Moon phase.
It is then possible for the Moon to pass through Earth&rsquo;s penumbral or umbral shadows thereby producing an eclipse.
There are three types of lunar eclipses: </p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Penumbral</strong> - Moon traverses Earth&rsquo;s penumbral shadow (Moon misses Earth&rsquo;s umbral shadow)</li>
<li><strong>Partial</strong> - Moon traverses Earth&rsquo;s penumbral and umbral shadows (Moon does not pass completely into Earth&rsquo;s umbra)</li>

<li><strong>Total</strong> - Moon traverses Earth&rsquo;s penumbral and umbral shadows (Moon passes completely into Earth&rsquo;s umbra)</li>
</li>
</ol>

<p class="quote">The <a href="../LEcat5/appearance.html">visual appearance</a> of these eclipse types differs dramatically from each other.</p>

The Moon passes completely within Earth&rsquo;s umbral shadow but misses the center of the umbra.

<h2 align="center">Calendar</h2>

<p class="quote">The Gregorian calendar is used for all dates from 1582 Oct 15 onwards.
Before that date, the Julian calendar is used.
For more information on this topic, see <a href="../SEhelp/calendar.html">Calendar Dates</a>.
The Julian calendar does not include the year 0.
Thus the year 1 BCE is followed by the year 1 CE (See: <a href="../SEhelp/dates.html"> BCE/CE Dating Conventions</a> ).
This is awkward for arithmetic calculations.
Years in this catalog are numbered astronomically and include the year 0.
Historians should note there is a difference of one year between astronomical dates and BCE dates.
Thus, the astronomical year 0 corresponds to 1 BCE, and astronomical year -1 corresponds to 2 BCE, etc..

<h2 align="center">Predictions</h2>

<p class="quote">Lunar eclipse predictions must take into account the <a href="../LEcat5/shadow.html">enlargement of Earth's shadows</a>.
In this <em>Catalog</em>, Earth's penumbral and umbral shadow sizes have been calculated using Danjon's enlargement method.</p>

<p class="quote">The coordinates of the Sun used in the predictions are based on the VSOP87 theory [Bretagnon and Francou, 1988].
The Moon's coordinates are based on the ELP-2000/82 theory [Chapront-Touze and Chapront, 1983].
For more information, see: <a href="../LEcat5/ephemeris.html">Solar and Lunar Ephemerides</a>.
The revised value used for the Moon's <a href="../LEcat5/secular.html"> secular acceleration </a> is n-dot = -25.858 arc-sec/cy*cy, as deduced from the Apollo lunar laser ranging experiment (Chapront, Chapront-Touze, and Francou, 2002).</p>

<p class="quote">The largest uncertainty in the eclipse predictions is caused by fluctuations in <a href="../SEhelp/rotation.html">Earth's rotation</a> due primarily to tidal friction of the Moon.
The resultant drift in apparent clock time is expressed as <a href="../LEcat5/deltat.html">&#916;T</a> and is determined as follows:</p>

<ol>
<li>pre-1950's: &#916;T calculated from empirical fits to historical records derived by Morrison and Stephenson (2004)</li>
<li>1955-2006: &#916;T obtained from published observations</li>

<li>Post-2006: &#916;T is extrapolated from current values weighted by the long term trend from tidal effects</li>
</ol>

<p class="quote">
A series of <a href="../LEcat5/deltatpoly.html">polynomial expressions</a> have been derived to simplify the evaluation of &#916;T for any time from -1999 to +3000.
The <a href="../LEcat5/uncertainty.html">uncertainty in &#916;T</a> over this period can be estimated from scatter in the measurements.
</p>
